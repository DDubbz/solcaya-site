The studio journal

How do you choose your first dance song?

Choose a first dance song that means something to both of you and feels comfortable to move to. Listen to the whole lyric, test the exact recording together, and agree on an opening and ending with your DJ or playback operator. A familiar favourite, an unexpected cover, or an instrumental can all work. The right choice does not need to impress the room; it needs to feel like yours.

A first dance can become a surprisingly difficult decision. A song sounds beautiful through headphones, yet feels too exposed when you imagine everyone watching. Another holds years of memories but opens with a long instrumental passage. The studio starts with the relationship, then considers how the recording will work in the room. That order keeps a personal moment from becoming a search for the most fashionable wedding song.

Where should you start if you do not have “your song”?

Start with listening rather than wedding lists. Each partner can bring a few records connected to a shared journey, an ordinary evening at home, or the feeling they want to carry into married life. Play them together without trying to choose immediately. Notice which ones make you both lean in.

If nothing overlaps, agree on a mood before a title: quiet and close, warm and playful, or openly celebratory. Then choose a small shortlist within that mood. A song does not need a long history with you to belong to you. It can become meaningful through this decision.

Do the lyrics matter more than the melody?

Both matter, but they do different work. The melody shapes how the moment feels; the lyric gives it a story. Listen beyond the chorus. A tender arrangement can accompany words about separation, regret, or a love that cannot last. That does not automatically disqualify a favourite, but it is worth making the choice consciously rather than discovering the meaning during rehearsal.

For a song in another language, read a reliable translation together and ask a fluent speaker about phrases that may carry a different meaning. If you love the music but not the story, an instrumental version may be a better fit. Silence between you is allowed; the record does not have to explain the entire relationship.

How can you tell whether a song will be easy to dance to?

Stand up and try it. Listen for a beat you can follow without concentrating on every step. A tempo number alone will not tell you whether the rhythm feels natural: phrasing, pauses, and changes in the arrangement also shape the dance.

If you want a simple embrace and sway, practise that rather than imagining choreography you do not intend to learn. If you are working with a dance instructor, share the actual recording before building a routine. A live performance, acoustic cover, and studio version of the same song may have different introductions and timing.

The studio's simplest test: if you stop thinking about the song and start noticing each other, it belongs on the shortlist.

How long should your first dance be?

There is no required duration. Published wedding advice varies: The Knot suggests a short excerpt of 60–90 seconds, while other guides favour a longer dance. Treat those suggestions as options, not etiquette. The useful question is how long you both want to be alone on the floor.

For couples who feel uneasy in the spotlight, the studio recommends trying a shorter passage that includes the lyric or chorus they love. For a rehearsed routine, let the choreography determine the length. You may also prefer the whole recording. Rehearse your chosen duration once before deciding; a favourite four-minute listen and a four-minute public dance are different experiences.

If you want a shortened version, ask your DJ or editor to preserve a complete musical phrase and give the ending room to land. Do not choose an arbitrary timestamp halfway through a lyric. Confirm whether the plan is a prepared edit, a live fade, or an invitation for guests to join you.

Should you choose the original, a cover, or an instrumental?

Choose the version you connect with, then check that it works in practice. An acoustic arrangement may make a familiar track feel more intimate. An instrumental can leave space for the moment without putting words in your mouths. The original may be the only version that carries the memory you want.

Do not approve a title alone. Save the artist, recording or album, and a reference link so your planner and DJ can identify the same version. Check for applause, spoken introductions, explicit lyrics, or a long ending. If an edit is needed, ask the person preparing it what is feasible before promising a specific transition to your choreographer.

What should you send your DJ or planner?

A small, precise brief is more useful than scattered messages. Share one final set of instructions with the person responsible for playback and the planner coordinating the moment:

  • Exact recording: song title, artist, version, and a reference link.
  • Opening: start from the beginning or an agreed cue; explain whether you walk onto the floor before the music begins.
  • Ending: full track, approved edit, fade, or guest invitation, with the agreed cue.
  • Final file: identify the approved version clearly and remove ambiguity around earlier drafts.
  • Operator: name who starts the track and who gives that person the signal.
  • Backup: confirm a permitted offline copy and test playback on the equipment being used, rather than depending on a streaming connection.

Once an edit is approved, rehearse to that file, not the unedited recording. If your photographer or videographer needs to anticipate a turn, dip, or guest entrance, share that cue through your planner. This is not about making the dance rigid. It is about keeping the technical decisions out of your thoughts when the music starts.

What if you would rather skip a formal first dance?

Then skip it, shorten it, or share it privately. You can invite everyone onto the floor from the start, dance together away from the reception, or let a meaningful song appear elsewhere in the evening. A tradition is useful when it serves the two of you, not when it creates a performance neither of you wants.

The studio approaches the first dance as one personal moment within the wider soundtrack. If you are considering a bespoke commission, include your chosen song, the exact version, and any playback or editing requirements in your inquiry so the scope can be agreed before work begins.

Where can you find more first-dance guidance?

For further listening and planning, explore The Knot's first-dance song guide and Gunther Sound's selection checklist. Their recommendations differ on duration; the studio's advice is to test the music together and choose deliberately, rather than treating any suggested length or tempo as a rule.
